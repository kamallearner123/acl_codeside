Start here: a sensor is a physical-to-electrical translator

A sensor responds to a physical quantity—light, heat, force, position or a chemical—and changes a resistance, voltage, current, charge or digital contact. A circuit conditions that change into a signal that can be measured, displayed, compared with a threshold or used to switch an actuator.

1. The quantity

Decide what is measured and its range: for example, temperature in °C or light level relative to a reference.

2. The transducer

Identify the sensor's output: resistance, a tiny current, generated voltage, capacitance or open/closed contact.

3. The conditioning

Use a divider, bridge, amplifier, filter or comparator to make a useful and stable signal.

4. The action

Use a properly rated transistor, MOSFET, relay or driver to operate a load; never ask a sensor to drive it directly.

Learning promise: every practical exercise can be completed with a breadboard, a safe low-voltage supply, basic components and a multimeter. The sensor-to-action circuits in this handbook do not use an MCU.

Resistive sensors: the simplest circuits to explore

Potentiometer and position

A potentiometer is a three-terminal adjustable divider. Its wiper voltage represents shaft or slider position. It is useful for position feedback, but loading the wiper changes the reading. A rotary potentiometer is not the same as a precision rotary encoder, which reports discrete movement.

Force-sensitive resistor and strain gauge

An FSR's resistance usually falls as applied force increases; it is suitable for qualitative pressure and presence, not calibrated weighing. A strain gauge changes resistance by a tiny amount and is normally placed in a Wheatstone bridge, then amplified by an instrumentation amplifier. A load cell is a bridge-based force transducer.

Soil and liquid conductivity

Two electrodes in wet soil or liquid form a crude conductivity sensor. Readings vary with salts, temperature, electrode spacing and corrosion. Do not leave DC across bare electrodes: use brief measurements or alternating excitation. For durable soil measurement prefer a capacitive probe.

Light and optical sensors

LDR: resistance generally decreases in brighter light. Use a divider for a threshold or light-level experiment; response is slow and part-to-part tolerance is large.

Photodiode: produces photocurrent, often tiny and approximately proportional to illumination. A reverse-biased photodiode with a transimpedance amplifier gives a fast, measurable voltage. Phototransistor: offers more output current but is generally slower and more variable.

IR reflective / break-beam: pair an IR LED with a phototransistor. Modulate or shield from ambient light for better reliability. Colour sensors use filtered photodiodes; flame detectors respond to a spectral band and must not be treated as safety-rated fire alarms.

Temperature and environmental sensors

NTC thermistor

Resistance falls as temperature rises. In a divider it produces a nonlinear voltage; use a lookup table or calibration for accurate temperature.

PTC and RTD

PTC resistance rises sharply or predictably over a range. Platinum RTDs such as Pt100/Pt1000 are stable and require careful lead and excitation design.

Thermocouple

Two dissimilar metals create a tiny voltage related to a temperature difference. Amplification and cold-junction compensation are essential.

Humidity

Capacitive humidity elements change capacitance; resistive types change impedance. Most require a specified AC or electronic interface.

For an NTC divider, calculate a safe excitation current and avoid self-heating. Calibrate against a known thermometer. A bare thermistor is not a fire-protection device.

Magnetic, proximity and position sensors

Reed switch: a sealed contact operated by a magnet. Simple door, float and rotation pickup; bounce can be filtered with an RC network or Schmitt trigger. Hall switch: an electronic output changes when magnetic field crosses a threshold; check whether it is open-collector and add a pull-up if required.

Inductive proximity: an oscillator coil detects metal through a change in losses; common industrial sensors contain their own oscillator and provide a switched output. Magnetoresistive: resistance changes with magnetic field and can measure angle or field with a suitable bridge/interface.

Position and level: potentiometers provide continuous position; float and tilt switches provide discrete states; capacitive and ultrasonic arrangements can sense a level without a moving contact.

Motion, distance, vibration and rotation

  • Accelerometer / gyroscope: MEMS sensors measure acceleration or angular rate, often as a digital serial output. A simple tilt-ball switch instead closes when its orientation changes.
  • Ultrasonic ranging: a transmitter emits a sound burst and a receiver detects the echo. A discrete implementation needs a burst oscillator, receiver amplifier, blanking/time measurement and threshold logic; many ready-made modules package these circuits but still do not require an MCU to experiment with their analog signals.
  • Vibration / piezo disc: mechanical stress produces a bipolar pulse. Use a high-value input resistor, clamp/protection and an amplifier/comparator; do not connect a piezo directly to a sensitive input without checking peak voltage.
  • Speed / rotation: a slotted optical interrupter, Hall sensor with a magnet, or reed switch can generate pulses. A 555/CMOS counter or frequency-to-voltage circuit can indicate rate without firmware.
  • Flow: turbine meters produce pulses; differential pressure and thermal flow sensors need their specified analog conditioning. Calibrate in the actual fluid and pipe configuration.

Sound, pressure, force and weight

Electret microphone: requires a bias resistor and produces a small AC signal. A transistor or op-amp preamplifier raises it to a useful level. Add a coupling capacitor and keep the input wiring short to reduce hum.

Piezo microphone / knock sensor: behaves as a high-impedance charge source. Use a buffer or high-input-impedance amplifier, protection, and a threshold stage for event detection.

Pressure transducers: piezoresistive diaphragm sensors commonly output a bridge signal. Capacitive pressure cells change capacitance; piezoelectric pressure sensors are better for dynamic events than static pressure.

Load cells: usually four strain gauges in a bridge, with a very small differential output. Stable excitation, instrumentation amplification, mechanical mounting and calibration weights are all important. Do not promise accurate weight from one resistor divider.

Chemical, gas, humidity and moisture sensors

Metal-oxide gas sensors (for example, MQ family): a heated element and sensing resistance respond to gases. They draw heater current, need warm-up, calibration and compensation; their broad response is not a selective or safety-certified gas alarm.

Electrochemical gas cells: can be selective and low-power, but their output is typically a very small current and the manufacturer specifies the bias and potentiostat circuit. pH electrodes have high impedance and need a very high-input-impedance amplifier; ordinary divider circuits are inappropriate.

Conductivity / soil probes: measure ionic conduction and are sensitive to contamination, temperature and electrode polarisation. Use AC excitation for long-term operation and calibrate against the target medium.

Humidity: capacitive elements require an AC measurement method; a bare capacitive element cannot be read as a DC resistor. Digital humidity modules include their own signal conditioning, but the underlying capacitance principle can be explored with an oscillator and frequency meter.

Safety note: never use a hobby gas sensor or this handbook's threshold circuits as the sole protection for people, buildings, combustion equipment or toxic-gas exposure.

Circuit cookbook: sensor signal to a useful decision

Comparator with an adjustable threshold

An LM393/LM358 comparator stage compares a sensor voltage to a reference made with a potentiometer. The output changes state when the sensor crosses the threshold. The LM393 output is open collector and needs a pull-up; check the part's supply and common-mode limits. Add positive feedback for hysteresis.

555 timer as oscillator, one-shot or pulse detector

A 555 can create a tone, periodic pulse, delay or PWM waveform. A sensor can alter its timing capacitor/resistor or trigger input. Use a CMOS 555 where low power or higher input impedance helps, and decouple the supply close to the IC. It is a timer, not a general-purpose analog-to-digital converter.

RC filter and noise control

For a first-order RC low-pass, the approximate cutoff is fc = 1 / (2πRC). Filtering can suppress contact bounce or electrical noise, but excessive filtering makes the response slow. Route sensor signals away from motor wires; use a common return thoughtfully.

Sensor-driven transistor switch

A comparator output or divider can drive a transistor through a resistor. Calculate base/gate drive and load current. For a coil, place a flyback diode across the coil; for a DC motor, use a suitably rated MOSFET/driver, diode and supply decoupling.

Actuators: turn a circuit decision into physical action

Indicator LED and lamp

LEDs need a series current-limiting resistor. Select resistor from supply voltage, LED forward voltage and desired current.

Buzzer and speaker

Active buzzers sound from DC; passive piezo buzzers need an alternating drive. A speaker needs an audio amplifier, not a sensor output pin.

Relay

A coil moves isolated contacts. Switch the coil with a transistor and flyback diode. Check coil voltage, contact ratings, isolation and inductive-load derating.

Solenoid and valve

Electromagnetic motion is usually on/off. Provide a correctly rated driver and suppression; a valve must match fluid, pressure and duty cycle.

DC motor and fan

Use a power MOSFET or motor driver, flyback path, current-rated supply and decoupling. A 555 can provide PWM speed control with no MCU.

Stepper motor

Needs a sequenced winding-current driver. Discrete logic or a dedicated stepper-driver IC can generate the sequence without firmware.

RC servo

Responds to repeated control pulses; a 555-based pulse generator can demonstrate position control. Supply the servo separately when required.

Heater / Peltier

Use a rated power switch, thermal fuse/limit, heatsinking and correctly rated supply. A Peltier device needs polarity and hot-side heat management.

Guided bench projects (no microcontroller)

  1. Dark-activated lamp: LDR divider → comparator with hysteresis → transistor → low-voltage LED lamp. Record switching thresholds in both directions.
  2. Thermostat and fan: NTC divider → adjustable comparator → MOSFET-driven small DC fan with flyback diode. Measure setpoint and hysteresis.
  3. Door-open alarm: reed contact → pull-up → 555 one-shot or oscillator → buzzer transistor. Compare normally-open and normally-closed wiring.
  4. Break-beam counter demonstrator: IR LED/phototransistor → Schmitt trigger → CMOS counter/display. Count objects by interrupting the beam; no firmware involved.
  5. Knock/vibration indicator: piezo disc → protected high-impedance amplifier → comparator → LED. Compare light and hard taps without treating it as a calibrated accelerometer.
  6. Water-level alarm: float switch → transistor/555 → buzzer. Build at safe extra-low voltage and keep the electronics physically separated from water.

For each build: draw the schematic, list the current path, calculate resistor values, simulate or breadboard, record expected versus measured voltages, identify failure modes, and explain how the sensor's physical property created the signal.

Safety, good measurement and glossary

  • Use a current-limited, isolated, extra-low-voltage bench supply (typically 3–12 V) for student breadboards. Never prototype mains circuits on a solderless breadboard.
  • Disconnect power before changing wiring. Check electrolytic capacitor polarity and LED/transistor pinouts. A motor can draw several times its running current at startup or stall.
  • Use the multimeter current range only in series; placing it directly across a supply can short the supply. Never measure resistance on a powered circuit.
  • Use a flyback diode for DC relay/solenoid coils and appropriate suppression for AC loads. Verify polarity and ratings; some drivers need a different clamp arrangement.
  • Do not expose students to unknown chemicals, gases, hot surfaces, laser beams or rotating machinery. Use benign classroom demonstrations and appropriate eye/thermal protection.
  • For this course, “without microcontrollers” means sensor experiments and actuation are built from analog/discrete circuits, timer ICs and dedicated driver ICs—not software-controlled MCU boards.
